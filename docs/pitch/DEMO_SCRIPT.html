Judge Q&A

1. "Why not just use GPT (or Claude) for this?"

We did. They're on the report card. GPT-5.6 Sol scored 0.90, second place. Even the top model, Claude Opus 5.5, raised false alarms on 17% of hazards that weren't there, and across all 14 models only 20% caught a towel bar used as a grab bar. We're not a model, we're the test. Without a guideline-derived answer key, nobody can tell whether any model is safe to point at a patient's home. We're model-agnostic, and the training packs make whichever model you use better.

2. "Is synthetic data realistic enough to matter?"

It's a controlled experiment, not a replacement for real homes. Each scene is a photoreal room where one edit changes about 2% of the pixels. Everything except the hazard is held constant, so a miss tells you exactly what the model can't see. Real photos can't isolate that. Images are also a clinically valid medium: OTs rating HOME FAST from photos agreed at AC1 0.91. Walkthrough mode already tests models in 360° worlds, and real-home validation is on the roadmap, where we measure the sim-to-real gap directly.

3. "How do you know the labels are right?"

The label exists before the image does, and a clinician signs off on it. Every rubric row cites a guideline line, and a clinician approves the rubric. Each scene is generated from one row. A vision-verify pass confirms the hazard is visible and nothing else changed; failed edits are dropped, and we report it: 4 of 35 edits failed, 3 of them measurement scenes that generators can't render reliably yet. The pixel diff localizes it, and the absent-feature and measurement scenes get a human spot check.

4. "What's the business model?"

Eval-as-a-service plus training packs. Home-health AI companies pay for a report card that proves their model works before a hospital pilot, and they re-run it on every release. They buy hard-example packs generated where their model fails. Hospitals and payers under CJR-X are the pull: they have to choose vendors, and "What's your HomeBench score on our guideline?" becomes the procurement question.

5. "What's the moat? Can't anyone generate images?"

Rendering isn't the moat. World Labs and NVIDIA already generate homes. The moat is the step from guideline to verified label: citing the guideline, getting clinician approval, verifying the edit, and getting a free box from the diff. Each new guideline adds a benchmark to the library, each model we test adds to the failure dataset, and whoever owns the benchmark becomes the procurement standard.

6. "Isn't this just for falls? How big is it?"

Falls are the first guideline, not the product. The same pipeline takes any guideline that can be checked visually: dementia home safety, pediatric home safety, wound photos. Fall detection comes later as a separate brief. Every guideline becomes a new benchmark and a new buyer.

Bonus: "Who else is doing this?"

Nobody owns clinical eval for the home. Lightwheel and World Labs run this loop for robots. TSHA benchmarks general safety hazards, not clinical guidelines. OmniFall covers the fall event, not the room. A 2026 systematic review says environmental factors are largely missing from AI fall-risk models.

Bonus: "What did you build today? What's HomeReady?"

HealthDojo was built today: the repo was created this morning. HomeReady is separate prior work and our customer #1. Its post-op fall-prevention guideline became HomeBench, our first 35-hazard benchmark.

Bonus: "How does AWS fit?"

13 of our 14 models run through one Bedrock Converse API: Nova Lite, Nova Pro, Nova 2 Lite, Llama 4, Qwen3-VL, Mistral, Pixtral, Gemma and more. The Nova models also take video natively, which is our path to walkthroughs. The report card is hosted on S3.
