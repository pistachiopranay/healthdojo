Judge Q&A

1. "Why can't vendors build this themselves?"

They can build a test. They can't grade themselves. A hospital won't pick a vendor on that vendor's own eval. Rare cases are the expensive part: a towel bar used as a grab bar caught only 20% of the time is exactly the case a vendor's real-photo set almost never contains, and synthetic homes give us that case with an exact label on demand. And only an independent party can put 14 vendors' models on the same answer key, so a hospital can compare them.

2. "Does any of this transfer to real homes?"

Not proven yet, and we won't claim it is. Synthetic scenes are a controlled experiment: one hazard changes, everything else is held constant, so a miss tells you exactly what the model can't judge. Real-home validation is next. HomeReady is customer zero, with OT-labeled real home photos, so we can measure the sim-to-real gap directly. Photos are a clinically valid medium: OTs rating HOME FAST from photos agreed at AC1 0.91.

3. "Opus scored 0.92. Isn't this solved?"

Detection is close. Judgment and search aren't. 0.92 is spotting. Adequacy is where models fail: towel bar as grab bar 20%, broken steps 36%, short handrail 57%. Even Opus raised false alarms on 17% of hazards that weren't there, and a nurse won't use a tool that cries wolf. In the walkthrough, where the model has to decide where to look, Opus found 3 of 4 and Nova Pro 1 of 4.

4. "An LLM wrote the rubric. What if it hallucinates?"

It can't add a line the guideline doesn't have. Every rubric row must quote its guideline line verbatim; we check the quote is a substring of the source and mark it if not. Then a clinician signs off before any scene is generated. Scene edits get a separate judge: 4 of 35 were rejected and dropped.

5. "Won't HealthBench, Scale or the labs commoditize this?"

They grade text. We grade the home. HealthBench-style evals are text conversations. We start from the guideline, write the label before the pixels, and test embodied behavior: where the model looks, not just what it says. The flywheel is ours: every failure we find becomes a harder scene. And the aim is for our rating to become the one hospitals use to choose vendors.

6. "Who pays, and how big is it?"

Buyers, not just vendors. AI vendors pay for eval-as-a-service before pilots and hard-example training packs after. Hospitals and payers under CJR-X pay for an independent rating to choose which AI to trust. Home robotics and smart-glasses makers need the same answer key for a new embodiment. And each guideline (falls, dementia, pressure injuries, medication, pediatrics) is a new simulator and a new buyer.

7. "You build HomeReady. Isn't that a conflict of interest?"

HomeReady is customer zero, graded like everyone else. It's why the simulator exists. Scoring is open and reproducible: the rubric, the labels and the grader are shared with every vendor, so anyone can re-run their score and check ours. HomeReady doesn't get a private answer key.

8. "Is this really cheaper than trials?"

It's before trials, not instead of them. The simulator catches the gaps you'd otherwise find on patients, months earlier and at a fraction of the cost, so the trial you do run tests a model that has already passed the obvious cases. Pilots still happen; fewer of them fail for reasons a simulator could have caught.
